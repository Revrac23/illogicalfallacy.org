Illogical Fallacy

A practical guide to recognizing, understanding, and avoiding logical fallacies.

Fallacies

Ad Hominem

Attacking the person making an argument instead of addressing the argument itself.

Ambiguity

Using an unclear or ambiguous statement to support a conclusion without resolving its possible meanings.

Amphiboly

Using a grammatically ambiguous statement in a way that exploits or ignores its possible interpretations.

Anecdotal Fallacy

Using a personal experience or isolated anecdote as sufficient evidence for a broader conclusion.

Appeal to Authority

Treating an authority's claim as sufficient evidence that the claim must be true.

Appeal to Emotion

Using an emotional response as a substitute for evidence supporting a conclusion.

Appeal to Expertise

Treating an expert's opinion as decisive evidence without considering whether the expertise, evidence, or consensus supports the claim.

Appeal to Fear

Using fear of a negative consequence to persuade someone to accept a conclusion without adequate evidence.

Appeal to Ignorance

Claiming that something must be true because it has not been proven false, or false because it has not been proven true.

Appeal to Nature

Assuming that something is good, safe, or desirable because it is natural, or bad because it is unnatural.

Appeal to Pity

Using sympathy or compassion as a substitute for evidence supporting a conclusion.

Appeal to Probability

Treating a probable possibility as though it were certain to be true.

Appeal to Tradition

Claiming that something is correct, valuable, or necessary simply because it has traditionally been done that way.

Bandwagon

Claiming that a belief or practice is correct because many people accept or follow it.

Base Rate Fallacy

Ignoring the general frequency of an event or condition when evaluating specific evidence about it.

Begging the Question

Using a premise that assumes the conclusion the argument is supposed to establish.

Burden of Proof

Failing to recognize that the person making a claim is generally responsible for providing evidence supporting it.

Cherry Picking

Selecting evidence that supports a conclusion while ignoring relevant evidence that contradicts it.

Circular Reasoning

Using a conclusion as support for itself, directly or through a chain of reasoning.

Composition

Assuming that what is true of individual parts must also be true of the whole.

Correlation Does Not Imply Causation

Assuming that because two things are correlated, one must have caused the other.

Division

Assuming that what is true of a whole must also be true of each individual part.

Domino Effect

Claiming that one event will trigger a chain of events without sufficient evidence that the chain will occur.

Equivocation

Using the same word or phrase with different meanings within the same argument.

False Cause

Incorrectly identifying the cause of an event or outcome.

False Dilemma

Presenting only two options when more possibilities exist.

Gambler's Fallacy

Believing that an independent random event is more or less likely because previous independent events had different outcomes.

Genetic Fallacy

Judging a claim solely by its origin rather than evaluating the claim itself.

Hasty Generalization

Drawing a broad conclusion from an insufficient or unrepresentative sample.

Hot-Hand Fallacy

Believing that a person is more likely to succeed again after a series of successful outcomes, even when the events are independent.

Loaded Question

Asking a question that contains an unproven or questionable assumption.

Moving the Goalposts

Changing the standard of evidence required after the original standard has been met.

No True Scotsman

Redefining a category to exclude counterexamples to a generalization.

Poisoning the Well

Discrediting a person or source in advance so that their future arguments will be dismissed regardless of their evidence.

Post Hoc

Assuming that because one event happened after another, the first event caused the second.

Red Herring

Introducing an irrelevant issue to distract from the argument being discussed.

Shifting the Burden of Proof

Demanding that others disprove a claim instead of providing evidence for the claim being made.

Slippery Slope

Claiming that one event will inevitably lead to extreme consequences without sufficient evidence.

Special Pleading

Applying a standard inconsistently by making an unsupported exception for a favored case.

Stereotyping

Applying a generalized belief about a group to an individual without sufficient evidence.

Straw Man

Misrepresenting someone's argument so that it is easier to attack.

Texas Sharpshooter

Selecting patterns or evidence after the fact and treating the selected pattern as if it had been predicted in advance.

Tu Quoque

Rejecting criticism by accusing the critic of the same behavior.

Whataboutism

Responding to criticism by pointing to another person's wrongdoing instead of addressing the original criticism.
