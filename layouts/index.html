Try One

Can you spot the problem?

“We shouldn’t listen to Maya’s proposal. She’s always late to meetings.”
Answer for Maya’s proposal

Maya’s punctuality tells us nothing about the merits of her proposal. Dismissing an argument based on an irrelevant criticism of the person making it is an ad hominem fallacy.

“Either we close the library on weekends or we have to double its budget.”
Answer for the library budget

The argument presents two options as if they were the only ones. The library could also shorten its weekend hours. Overlooking alternatives this way creates a false dilemma.

“The two buses I took this week were late. Buses on this route are always late.”
Answer for the late buses

Two rides give us too little evidence to judge how often buses on the route arrive late. Drawing a broad conclusion from too few observations is a hasty generalization.

Why this site exists

Arguments matter.

Logical fallacies are patterns of reasoning that can make an argument appear stronger than it really is. Learning to recognize them makes it easier to evaluate claims, identify weak reasoning, and have better conversations.

Each entry in this library explains what a fallacy is, shows an example, and describes how to recognize it in practice.
